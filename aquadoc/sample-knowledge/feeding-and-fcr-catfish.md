# Feed Conversion Ratio and Feeding Management in African Catfish

*Sample knowledge document for local development.*

This file exists so a developer can exercise the ingestion → approval →
retrieval → answer path without sourcing a real publication first. It is
written from general aquaculture practice and is **not** an authoritative
source. Ingest it as evidence level `C` at most, and do not approve it in any
environment that serves real farmers.

## 1. Feed Conversion Ratio

Feed conversion ratio (FCR) compares the mass of feed supplied to the mass of
fish gained over the same period:

    FCR = total feed supplied (kg) / total weight gain (kg)

A lower FCR indicates more efficient feed use. An FCR of 1.2 means 1.2 kg of
feed produced 1 kg of growth. Well-managed African catfish (*Clarias
gariepinus*) grow-out systems commonly operate between 1.0 and 1.5.

FCR is a whole-period measure. A single day's figure is dominated by sampling
error and by the lag between feeding and measurable growth, so it should be
computed over a production cycle or at least several weeks.

### Causes of a rising FCR

- Overfeeding, where uneaten pellets settle and decompose
- Pellet size mismatched to mouth size, increasing waste
- Poor water quality suppressing appetite and growth
- Underestimated mortality inflating the assumed standing biomass
- Feed that has degraded through age, damp storage, or heat

## 2. Temperature and Feed Intake

Fish are ectotherms: metabolic rate tracks water temperature. The Q10
coefficient describes how much a biological rate changes per 10 °C. For warm
water finfish a Q10 near 2.0 is a common working value, meaning metabolic rate
roughly doubles per 10 °C rise within the tolerable range.

African catfish feed actively between roughly 26 °C and 30 °C. Below about
22 °C, appetite falls markedly. Reduced intake at low temperature is an
expected thermal response and is not by itself evidence of disease.

Above roughly 32 °C two effects compound: oxygen demand rises while oxygen
solubility in water falls. Heavy feeding at high temperature therefore adds
oxygen demand exactly when the least oxygen is available.

## 3. Feeding Rate by Size

Daily feed is commonly expressed as a percentage of standing biomass. Smaller
fish eat proportionally more:

| Average weight | Daily feed (% of biomass) |
|---|---|
| Up to 5 g | 10% |
| 5–20 g | 7% |
| 20–50 g | 5% |
| 50–150 g | 3.5% |
| 150–300 g | 2.5% |
| Above 300 g | 1.8% |

These are starting points, not prescriptions. Adjust against observed feeding
response, water temperature, and water quality.

## 4. Reduced Feeding Response

When fish eat less than expected, the ordinary explanations are more common
than disease. Check in roughly this order:

1. **Water temperature** — below the active feeding range, reduced intake is
   expected.
2. **Dissolved oxygen** — low oxygen suppresses feeding before it produces any
   other visible sign. This is often the first symptom a farmer can observe.
3. **Recent handling** — grading, sampling, or transport suppresses appetite
   for one to three days.
4. **Feed quality and pellet size** — check storage conditions and that pellet
   size still matches the fish.
5. **Ammonia and nitrite** — elevated levels stress fish and reduce intake.
6. **Disease** — consider once the above are excluded, particularly alongside
   lesions, unusual swimming, gill changes, or rising mortality.

A single quiet feeding is weak evidence. A trend across several feedings, or
reduced intake together with any other sign, is worth acting on.

## 5. Water Quality Reference Ranges

General ranges for warm water pond culture:

| Parameter | Comfort range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 26–30 °C | Growth slows below 22 °C |
| pH | 6.5–8.5 | High pH raises un-ionised ammonia toxicity |
| Dissolved oxygen | Above 5 mg/L | Below 3 mg/L is acutely dangerous |
| Total ammonia | Below 0.5 mg/L | Toxicity rises with pH and temperature |
| Nitrite | Below 0.5 mg/L | Impairs oxygen transport in blood |
| Turbidity | Below 30 NTU | Higher suggests solids or algal load |

African catfish tolerate low oxygen better than most cultured species because
they can breathe air. That tolerance is a survival margin, not a growth
margin: fish surviving in poor water still grow slowly and convert feed poorly.

## 6. Measurement Before Intervention

Where a parameter has not been measured, it is unknown. It is not normal.

An unmeasured parameter cannot be excluded as a cause, and stating that water
quality "looks fine" on the basis of temperature alone is not supportable when
pH and dissolved oxygen were never measured. Measure first, then act.
